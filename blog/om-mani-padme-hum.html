Mantra Guide

Om Mani Padme Hum: Meaning, Benefits & How to Chant

A complete guide to the world's most beloved mantra of compassion — its meaning, symbolism, and practice.

Carved into prayer stones across the Himalayas, fluttering on prayer flags, spun in prayer wheels, and whispered by millions of devotees from Tibet to the modern West — Om Mani Padme Hum is perhaps the most famous and beloved mantra in the world. This six-syllable mantra of compassion holds, according to tradition, the entire essence of the Buddha's teaching within it.

The Om Mani Padme Hum Mantra

The mantra, in Sanskrit (Devanagari):

ॐ मणि पद्मे हूँ

In transliteration:

oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ

Pronunciation: In the Tibetan tradition it is pronounced Om Mani Peme Hung. The Sanskrit pronunciation is Om Ma-ni Pad-me Hum. Both are widely used; the Tibetan pronunciation is the most common in practice worldwide.

This is the mantra of Avalokiteshvara (Chenrezig in Tibetan) — the bodhisattva who embodies the compassion of all the Buddhas. The Dalai Lama is considered an emanation of Avalokiteshvara, which is part of why this mantra holds such a central place in Tibetan Buddhism. Its first textual appearance is in the Karandavyuha Sutra, a Mahayana Buddhist text from around the 4th or 5th century, where it is described as containing the entirety of the Buddha's teaching.

Om Mani Padme Hum Meaning

The literal translation is "the jewel in the lotus" — but as with all great mantras, the literal meaning is only the surface. The words break down as:

  • Om (ॐ) — the sacred primordial sound; represents the body, speech, and mind of the practitioner, and their potential to be transformed into the pure body, speech, and mind of an enlightened being.
  • Mani (मणि) — "jewel"; symbolises compassion, loving-kindness, and the altruistic intention to attain enlightenment for the benefit of all beings (bodhicitta). Just as a jewel is precious and wish-fulfilling, compassion transforms suffering into contentment.
  • Padme (पद्मे) — "lotus"; symbolises wisdom. Just as a lotus grows pure and unstained from muddy water, wisdom blooms pure even from the confusion of ordinary existence.
  • Hum (हूँ) — represents indivisibility, the union of compassion and wisdom; signifies the completion of the mantra and the enlightened mind itself.

Taken together, the mantra points to the heart of the Buddhist path: the union of compassion (the jewel) and wisdom (the lotus), which together lead to enlightenment. To recite Om Mani Padme Hum is to invoke the compassionate presence of Avalokiteshvara and to cultivate these same qualities within oneself.

The Six Syllables and the Six Perfections

In the Tibetan tradition, each of the six syllables is understood to help perfect one of the six paramitas — the six transcendent virtues that carry a practitioner toward enlightenment. As the revered master Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche taught:

  • Om — perfects the practice of generosity (dana)
  • Ma — perfects the practice of pure ethics (sila)
  • Ni — perfects the practice of tolerance and patience (kshanti)
  • Pad — perfects the practice of perseverance (virya)
  • Me — perfects the practice of concentration (dhyana)
  • Hum — perfects the practice of wisdom (prajna)

In this way, the recitation of these six syllables is understood to gradually perfect all six virtues of the enlightened path — from generosity to wisdom — which is why the tradition holds that the entire Dharma is contained within this single short mantra.

The Six Syllables and the Six Realms

In Buddhist cosmology, the six syllables are also understood to help close the doors to rebirth in the six realms of cyclic existence (samsara) — the god realm, demi-god realm, human realm, animal realm, hungry ghost realm, and hell realm. Each syllable is said to purify the specific negative emotion that leads to suffering in each realm — pride, jealousy, desire, ignorance, greed, and anger respectively — transforming these afflictions into wisdom and compassion.

Benefits of Chanting Om Mani Padme Hum

The mantra is traditionally associated with a wide range of benefits:

  • Cultivating compassion — its central purpose. Repeated recitation is understood to awaken and strengthen loving-kindness and compassion (bodhicitta) toward all beings.
  • Purification — the mantra is held to purify negative karma, afflictive emotions, and the mental patterns that cause suffering.
  • Calming the mind — the rhythmic repetition has a settling, centering effect, quieting mental agitation and creating inner peace.
  • Focus and mindfulness — reciting the mantra as a meditation trains concentration and present-moment awareness.
  • Accumulating merit — in Buddhist understanding, sincere recitation generates positive karma that supports spiritual growth.
  • Spiritual progress — at the deepest level, the mantra is a complete path — cultivating the compassion and wisdom that together lead toward enlightenment.

These are traditional understandings of the mantra's significance within the Buddhist tradition, valued by practitioners across many centuries and cultures.

How to Chant Om Mani Padme Hum

A simple approach to practice:

  1. Sit comfortably with a straight spine, in a quiet place.
  2. Take a few slow breaths to settle the mind.
  3. Bring to mind the intention of compassion — the wish for all beings to be free from suffering.
  4. Recite the mantra slowly and clearly: Om Mani Padme Hum (or Om Mani Peme Hung), letting each syllable resonate.
  5. As you chant, contemplate its meaning — the union of compassion and wisdom — and sense the vibration of the sound.
  6. Count your repetitions, traditionally in rounds of 108 on a mala.

The mantra can be recited aloud, whispered, or repeated silently in the mind. It can also be practised while walking, while turning a prayer wheel, or at any moment during daily life. What matters most is the motivation of compassion and the sincerity of the practice.

How Many Times Should You Chant It?

The mantra is traditionally chanted in multiples of 108 — one full round of a mala. Many practitioners commit to reciting it a set number of times each day; some traditions recommend 108, others 1,000 or more for dedicated practice. In Tibet, elderly practitioners are known to recite it many thousands of times daily, often continuously throughout the day. As with all mantra practice, consistency matters more than volume — a steady daily practice is more valuable than occasional intensive sessions.

Prayer Wheels and Prayer Flags

Om Mani Padme Hum is uniquely associated with two beloved Tibetan Buddhist practices. The prayer wheel is a cylinder inscribed with the mantra, often containing thousands or millions of printed copies of it inside. Turning the wheel with a gentle rhythm while reciting the mantra is understood to generate the same merit as reciting each mantra inside it. Prayer flags carry the mantra printed on cloth; as the wind moves them, tradition holds that the mantra's blessing of compassion is carried out into the world. Both practices reflect the same beautiful idea — that compassion, once set in motion, radiates outward to benefit all beings.

A Mantra Beyond Boundaries

While Om Mani Padme Hum is specifically a Tibetan Buddhist mantra, its opening syllable Om connects it to the broader family of sacred sound that spans the Buddhist, Hindu, Jain, and yogic traditions alike. Om is the universal seed-sound from which countless mantras across these traditions begin. In this sense, Om Mani Padme Hum sits within a vast, shared heritage of contemplative sound practice — even as it carries its own distinct meaning and lineage within Mahayana and Tibetan Buddhism.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Om Mani Padme Hum mean?

Literally, "the jewel in the lotus." More deeply, it represents the union of compassion (the jewel, Mani) and wisdom (the lotus, Padme) that together lead to enlightenment. It is the mantra of Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion.

Is it Om Mani Padme Hum or Om Mani Peme Hung?

They are the same mantra. "Om Mani Padme Hum" is the Sanskrit pronunciation; "Om Mani Peme Hung" is the Tibetan pronunciation, which is the most common in practice worldwide. Both are correct.

What religion is Om Mani Padme Hum from?

It is a Buddhist mantra, most central to Mahayana and Tibetan Buddhism. It first appears in the Karandavyuha Sutra, a Mahayana text from around the 4th–5th century, and is the mantra of the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara (Chenrezig).

How many times should I chant Om Mani Padme Hum?

Traditionally in multiples of 108 — one round of a mala. Many practitioners recite it 108 times daily; dedicated practitioners may recite it thousands of times. Consistency matters more than the number.

What are the six syllables of Om Mani Padme Hum?

Om, Ma, Ni, Pad, Me, Hum. Each is understood to help perfect one of the six virtues (generosity, ethics, patience, perseverance, concentration, and wisdom) and to purify the afflictive emotions that cause suffering.
