Mantra Guide

How to Correctly Chant the Maha Mrityunjaya Mantra

The Sanskrit, the meaning, precise pronunciation, common mistakes, and how to practise the great mantra of Shiva.

The Maha Mrityunjaya Mantra — the "great death-conquering mantra" — is one of the most powerful and revered mantras in the Vedic tradition, addressed to Lord Shiva as Tryambaka, the three-eyed one. It comes from the Rigveda (7.59.12) and is chanted for healing, protection, and release from fear. But its power is best realised when it is chanted correctly — with accurate pronunciation, proper vowel length, and understanding. This guide breaks the mantra down word by word and syllable by syllable, flags the most common mistakes, and shows you how to practise.

The Mantra

In Sanskrit (Devanagari):

ॐ त्र्यम्बकं यजामहे सुगन्धिं पुष्टिवर्धनम् ।
उर्वारुकमिव बन्धनान्मृत्योर्मुक्षीय मामृतात् ॥

In transliteration (IAST):

Oṁ Tryambakaṁ Yajāmahe Sugandhiṁ Puṣṭivardhanam
Urvārukam-iva Bandhanān Mṛtyormukṣīya Māmṛtāt

A note on "Om": the original Rigvedic verse does not include Om — it is a customary devotional prefix added by most traditions. You can chant with or without it, following your lineage.

Word-by-Word Meaning

Understanding each word makes correct chanting far easier, because you know where one word ends and the next begins:

  • Tryambakam — "the three-eyed one," Rudra / Shiva.
  • Yajāmahe — "we worship / we honour" (note: plural, "we").
  • Sugandhim — "the fragrant one," sweet-scented.
  • Puṣṭivardhanam — "the increaser of nourishment and well-being."
  • Urvārukam — a ripe gourd or cucumber-like fruit.
  • Iva — "like, as."
  • Bandhanāt — "from the stem / from the bond."
  • Mṛtyoḥ — "from death."
  • Mukṣīya — "may I be freed" (note: singular, "I").
  • Mā Amṛtāt — "not from immortality" — i.e. released from death, but not from the deathless, the eternal.

The sense: "We worship the three-eyed Lord, fragrant and nourishing. As a ripe cucumber is freed from its stem, may I be freed from death — but not from immortality." It is a prayer for liberation from the fear and bondage of death, like a fruit that detaches naturally when ripe.

Syllable-by-Syllable Pronunciation

Use these English approximations as a starting point. Keep long vowels (ā, ī) clearly longer than short ones — this is the single most important rule in Sanskrit chanting:

  • tryam·ba·kam — try-UM-ba-kam
  • ya·jā·ma·he — ya-JAA-ma-heh
  • su·gan·dhim — su-GUN-dhim
  • puṣ·ṭi·var·dha·nam — push-ṭi-VAR-dha-nam
  • ur·vā·ru·kam — ur-VAA-ru-kam
  • i·va — i-va
  • ban·dha·nāt — BUN-dha-naat
  • mṛt·yoḥ — MRIT-yoh
  • muk·ṣī·ya — muk-SHEE-ya
  • mā·mṛ·tāt — maa-mri-TAAT

Common Mistakes to Avoid

These are the errors that most often creep in — correcting them sharply improves your chanting:

  • Lengthening "muk" in Mukṣīya. The u is short; the ī is long. Many recordings stretch the wrong vowel.
  • Softening the aspirated "dh" in sugandhim and vardhanam. It is an aspirated "d-h," not a plain "d."
  • Flattening the retroflex sounds (ṣ, ṭ) in puṣṭi — these are pronounced with the tongue curled back, distinct from plain s and t.
  • Inserting an extra vowel between puṣṭi and vardhanam — keep it joined: puṣṭivardhanam.
  • Ending "yajāmahe" like English "hay." The final e is a long, pure vowel.
  • Rushing vowel length. When set to music, long and short vowels can blur — chant slowly enough to keep them distinct.
  • Mispronouncing ṛ in mṛtyoḥ. "Mrit" is only an approximation of the vocalic ṛ; listen to a careful reciter.

How to Practise

  1. Sit comfortably with a straight spine, eyes closed, and take a few slow breaths to settle.
  2. Begin slowly — accuracy before speed. It's better to chant it correctly a few times than quickly many times.
  3. Keep attention on the sound and its meaning as you chant.
  4. Chant a fixed number of repetitions — traditionally 108, counted on a mala or a counter — or for a set time.
  5. When finished, sit quietly for a moment before returning to activity.

How Many Times and When

The traditional count is 108 repetitions — one round of a japa mala. Many practitioners chant it each morning. The most auspicious time is the pre-dawn window of Brahma Muhurta, when the mind is naturally quiet. Monday and Mondays of the month of Shravan are traditionally associated with Shiva, and Maha Shivaratri is considered an especially powerful time for this mantra.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to include "Om" before the mantra?

The original Rigvedic verse does not include Om — it's a customary devotional prefix most traditions add. You can chant with or without it; follow your lineage or teacher. Both are widely practised.

What is the most important thing to get right?

Vowel length. Sanskrit distinguishes short and long vowels (a vs ā, i vs ī), and getting these right matters more than speed. Chant slowly enough to keep each vowel distinct, and the rest follows.

Can anyone chant the Maha Mrityunjaya Mantra?

Yes. It is a widely and openly chanted mantra that anyone can practise with sincerity and respect. Some traditions offer formal initiation, but none is required to begin chanting it devotionally.

How many times should I chant it?

The traditional count is 108 repetitions, often each morning. Shorter counts of 11, 21, or 27 are also common, or three times before an important undertaking. Consistency and correct pronunciation matter more than the number.

Is it okay to chant it silently?

Yes — silent mental repetition (manasa japa) is widely practised. For learning correct pronunciation, though, chanting aloud or whispered first helps you train the sounds accurately before moving to silent practice.
