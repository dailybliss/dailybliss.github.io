Om (Aum): Meaning, Significance & How to Chant

The foundational sound of the Vedic tradition — its meaning, the three syllables, and how to practise it.

Om — written as Aum in its original Sanskrit form, and represented by the sacred symbol ॐ — is the most fundamental sound in the Vedic and yogic tradition. It appears at the beginning of almost every Sanskrit mantra, in the opening verses of the Upanishads, and at the start and close of yoga and meditation sessions worldwide. This guide covers what Om actually means, what the three syllables represent, the benefits of chanting it, and how to practise it as part of a daily routine.

The Om (Aum) Symbol and Sound

In Sanskrit (Devanagari):

ॐ

In transliteration:

Aum

Pronounced: A-U-M — three distinct sounds blending into one. The "A" begins open and deep at the back of the throat; the "U" rolls forward along the palate; the "M" closes at the lips as a sustained nasal hum. A brief moment of silence follows — itself considered the fourth, most subtle dimension of Om.

The spelling "Om" became more common in the West simply because "Au" visually reads as "O" — but "Aum" is the original Sanskrit spelling and gives a more accurate sense of how the sound is formed and what it contains.

Om's Origin — The Mandukya Upanishad

Om is dealt with most directly in the Mandukya Upanishad — a remarkably brief text (just twelve verses) devoted entirely to explaining the significance of this one syllable. Its opening verse states plainly: Om is all this — what has been, what is, and what shall be. The Upanishad teaches that Om encompasses the totality of existence: past, present, and future; the manifest and the unmanifest; all sound and all silence.

Beyond the Mandukya Upanishad, Om appears throughout the Vedas, at the beginning and end of chapters of the Upanishads, in the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali (where it is described in Sutra 1.27 as the word that denotes the divine), and across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions. It is one of very few sacred sounds genuinely shared across the major traditions of Indian philosophy.

The Meaning of the Three Syllables

Each of the three sounds of Aum carries a specific meaning, with the silence at the end forming a fourth dimension:

  • A (ahh) — creation, the beginning of all things, the waking state of consciousness (Jagrat). The sound begins open, arising from the back of the throat, vibrating in the chest.
  • U (ooh) — preservation, the middle of existence, the dream state of consciousness (Svapna). The sound rolls forward, vibrating in the throat and palate.
  • M (mmm) — dissolution, transformation, the deep-sleep state of consciousness (Sushupti). The lips close gently; the vibration resonates in the skull.
  • Silence (◌̣) — the fourth state, Turiya — the transcendent witness beyond waking, dreaming, and sleep. The Mandukya Upanishad considers this the deepest dimension of Om: the silence that contains everything and is itself the nature of pure awareness.

Together, A-U-M represents the complete cycle of existence — creation, preservation, and dissolution — and the awareness that witnesses it all. This is why Om is understood not as a word with a conventional dictionary meaning but as a sound that is what it describes: the fundamental vibration of which all other sounds and all of creation are variations.

Why Om Begins Every Mantra

Almost every Sanskrit mantra begins with Om — the Gayatri Mantra (Om bhur bhuvaḥ svaḥ…), the Maha Mrityunjaya Mantra (Om tryambakaṃ…), Om Namah Shivaya, and countless others. This is intentional: Om is understood as the seed-sound (bija) from which all language and all mantras arise. Beginning a mantra with Om is understood as aligning with the primordial vibration before entering the specific prayer or intention that follows — like tuning an instrument to the correct frequency before playing.

In yoga classes, three Oms are traditionally chanted together at the beginning and close of a session for the same reason: to create a shared vibrational field, settle the mind, and mark a transition from ordinary activity to contemplative practice.

Benefits of Chanting Om

Om chanting is one of the most studied forms of mantra practice in modern research. The benefits reported across both traditional teaching and contemporary studies include:

  • Calming the nervous system — the sustained vibration of the "M" sound is understood to stimulate the vagus nerve, which governs the parasympathetic (rest and digest) nervous system, shifting the body from stress toward relaxation. Studies suggest a measurable deactivation of the limbic system — the brain's stress-response centre — during Om chanting, similar in profile to vagus nerve stimulation used in clinical settings.
  • Reducing stress and anxiety — regular practice has been shown to reduce cortisol levels, lower blood pressure and heart rate, and improve emotional resilience.
  • Improving focus and concentration — the single-pointed attention required to follow the sound through its three phases trains the mind in a way that transfers to other tasks.
  • Preparing the mind for meditation — chanting Om before sitting in meditation settles the breath, quiets mental activity, and creates a natural transition into deeper practice.
  • Purifying the environment — the vibrational quality of sustained Om chanting, particularly in a group, is traditionally held to create a calm, clear, and positive atmosphere in the space where it is practiced.
  • Connection to tradition — chanting Om places individual practice within a lineage of practitioners stretching back thousands of years across multiple traditions — a sense of continuity that many practitioners describe as itself grounding and settling.

How to Chant Om (Aum)

A simple, effective approach:

  1. Sit comfortably with a straight spine, eyes closed.
  2. Take one slow, deep breath to settle.
  3. Inhale fully. On the exhale, begin sounding A — open, from the back of the throat, vibrating in the chest. Let it naturally transition into U, rolling forward and vibrating in the throat and palate. Close the lips gently into M — a sustained hum that vibrates in the skull and crown.
  4. When the breath is complete, let the sound dissolve into silence. Rest there for a moment before the next breath.
  5. Repeat — typically 3, 9, or 108 times.

The key is unhurried attention: follow the vibration as it moves through the body, from chest to throat to skull to silence. Om does not need to be chanted loudly — a soft, steady tone is often more effective than volume for the internal resonance it creates.

How Many Times and When

Three repetitions is the traditional minimum — used to open and close yoga sessions. Nine, 21, or 108 repetitions are common for dedicated practice. As with all mantra practice, consistency matters more than volume: three Oms chanted with full attention every morning is more valuable than an occasional long session.

The best time to chant Om is the pre-dawn window of Brahma Muhurta — when the mind is naturally undistracted and the environment is quiet. Sunrise and sunset are also traditional times. Om can equally be chanted silently, as a form of mental japa, at any moment during the day.

Om Across Traditions

While Om is most closely associated with Hinduism and yoga, its presence extends across several traditions. It is used in Buddhist and Jain ritual and meditation. Scholars have observed phonetic parallels with the liturgical "Amen" of Christian and Jewish tradition, and "Amin" in Islam — though these are considered analogous rather than derived. In Sikhism, "Omkar" describes qualities of the divine. The observation that most human languages have words incorporating the A-U-M sounds (since they span the full range of the vocal tract from open throat to closed lips) has led many teachers to describe Om as genuinely universal — not by cultural agreement but by acoustic reality.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Om mean?

Om (Aum) is the primordial sound of the universe — the vibration from which all of creation is understood to arise. Its three syllables (A-U-M) represent creation, preservation, and dissolution, and the three states of consciousness (waking, dream, deep sleep), with a fourth dimension of silence representing pure awareness.

What is the difference between Om and Aum?

They are the same sound. "Aum" is the original Sanskrit spelling, more accurately capturing the three distinct sounds (A, U, M) that make up the syllable. "Om" became the more common Western spelling because "Au" visually resembles "O." Both refer to the same sacred sound.

Why does every mantra begin with Om?

Om is considered the seed-sound (bija) from which all language and all mantras arise. Beginning a mantra with Om aligns the practice with the fundamental vibration of existence before entering the specific intention of the mantra that follows.

How many times should Om be chanted?

Three repetitions is the traditional minimum. Nine, 21, or 108 repetitions are common for dedicated daily practice. Consistency and attention matter more than the number.

Can Om be chanted silently?

Yes — silent mental repetition (manasa japa) of Om is widely practiced and considered highly effective, particularly as a continuous background during meditation or during daily activity.

Is Om only for Hindus?

Om originates in the Vedic tradition and is most fully elaborated in Hindu philosophy, but it is used across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions, and many practitioners across various backgrounds incorporate it into their meditation practice. It is typically approached with respect for its origins while being open to sincere practitioners of any background.
