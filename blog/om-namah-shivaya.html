Mantra Guide

Om Namah Shivaya: Meaning, Benefits & How to Chant

A complete guide to the Panchakshara Mantra — its meaning, the five elements, benefits, and daily practice.

Om Namah Shivaya is one of the most widely chanted mantras in the Vedic and yogic tradition. Known as the Panchakshara Mantra — the five-syllable mantra — it is dedicated to Lord Shiva and has been repeated by yogis, saints, and householders for thousands of years. This guide covers the full mantra, its word-by-word meaning, its connection to the five elements, its benefits, and how to practise it daily.

The Om Namah Shivaya Mantra

The mantra, in Sanskrit (Devanagari):

ॐ नमः शिवाय

In transliteration (IAST):

oṃ namaḥ śivāya

Pronunciation: Aum Na-mah Shi-vaa-ya — the "Aum" begins deep and slow; the "Shi" is soft, not a hard "Sh"; the "vaa-ya" ends with a long "aa" sound.

Om Namah Shivaya is the central mantra of the Sri Rudram (Namakam), found in the Krishna Yajurveda (Taittiriya Samhita 4.5). It is also referenced in the Shiva Purana, which describes it as the seed of all Shaiva knowledge. It is among the oldest mantras in continuous daily use — chanted by Shaiva pandits, yogis, and devotees across India and increasingly across the world.

Om Namah Shivaya Meaning

Word by word:

  • Om (Aum) — the primordial sound of the universe; the sound of creation itself.
  • Namah — "I bow," "I salute," "I surrender." In Sanskrit, namah literally means "not mine" — a release of ego and a gesture of surrender to what is greater.
  • Shivaya — "to Shiva," the auspicious one; the pure consciousness that is the silent witness behind all creation.

Together: "I bow to Shiva" — or, more deeply: "I surrender to the divine consciousness within." At its deepest level, the mantra is understood not as a prayer directed outward to an external deity, but as a recognition of the Shiva-nature — pure awareness — within the chanter's own heart.

The Five Syllables and the Five Elements

The "Panchakshara" (five-syllable) mantra refers to the core five syllables: Na — Ma — Shi — Va — Ya. Each syllable is traditionally associated with one of the five elements (Pancha Bhoota) that make up all of creation — and the human body:

  • Na — Earth (Prithvi)
  • Ma — Water (Jala)
  • Shi — Fire (Agni)
  • Va — Air (Vayu)
  • Ya — Ether / Space (Akasha)

Since Shiva is understood as the master of the five elements, chanting Om Namah Shivaya is traditionally understood as harmonizing all five elements within the body and in the environment — bringing balance, stillness, and clarity.

Benefits of Chanting Om Namah Shivaya

The mantra is traditionally chanted for a range of purposes:

  • Calming the mind — its meditative rhythm naturally slows the breath and anchors attention, quieting mental noise and restlessness.
  • Release of stress and anxiety — sustained chanting creates a "soft reset" for the nervous system, helping dissolve accumulated tension.
  • Preparation for meditation — because it quiets the mind effectively, Om Namah Shivaya is widely used as a bridge into deeper meditation practice.
  • Dissolving ego and emotional blockages — the mantra's theme of surrender (namah — "not mine") is itself a practice of releasing attachment, anger, and fear.
  • Spiritual growth — sustained practice is understood in the Shaiva tradition as a direct path toward self-realization — the recognition of one's own Shiva-nature.
  • Protection and well-being — traditionally invoked for protection, healing, and overall well-being of the chanter and their household.

These are traditional understandings of the mantra's significance within the Vedic and yogic traditions, valued by practitioners across generations and cultures.

How to Chant Om Namah Shivaya

There are three traditional modes of chanting, each considered to have its own quality:

  • Vachika japa — chanting aloud. Helps focus the mind and is said to purify the surroundings through sound vibration.
  • Upanshu japa — whispering, with only the lips moving. Deepens concentration while keeping the sound inward.
  • Manasa japa — mental chanting, with no movement of lips or voice. Considered the most subtle and, in many traditions, the most powerful — the mind merges directly with the mantra.

A simple daily approach:

  1. Sit comfortably with a straight spine, facing north or east if possible.
  2. Take a few slow, deep breaths to settle the mind.
  3. Begin chanting at a gentle, unhurried pace — one repetition per slow breath.
  4. Count your repetitions using a rudraksha mala (108 beads) or a counter.
  5. When finished, sit quietly for one to two minutes. Let the vibration settle.

How Many Times Should You Chant It?

Traditionally, 108 repetitions — one full round of a japa mala — is the standard daily practice. At a meditative pace of roughly one mantra per slow breath, 108 repetitions takes around three to four minutes. Some practitioners chant 1008 repetitions (ten malas) on Mondays, which are traditionally associated with Lord Shiva, or on Maha Shivaratri. Consistency matters more than volume — a steady daily round of 108 is more valuable than occasional large counts.

The Best Time to Chant

Om Namah Shivaya can be chanted at any time — the mantra itself is considered auspicious in any moment. However, certain times are traditionally considered especially favourable:

  • Brahma Muhurta — the pre-dawn window before sunrise, approximately 96 minutes before dawn. This is the most auspicious time for all mantra practice in the Vedic tradition. The mind is naturally calm, undistracted, and receptive. See our guide on Brahma Muhurta for more.
  • Sunrise and sunset — the junction times (sandhya kala) between day and night are considered especially powerful for Shiva-related practice.
  • Monday — the day of the week traditionally associated with Lord Shiva. A Monday practice is considered especially auspicious, particularly during the Shravana (Sawan) month.
  • Maha Shivaratri — the great night of Shiva; the single most auspicious occasion for sustained Om Namah Shivaya chanting.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Om Namah Shivaya mean?

Literally, "I bow to Shiva." More deeply, it is understood as a surrender of the ego to pure divine consciousness — the Shiva-nature that is the silent witness within every being.

What is the Panchakshara Mantra?

Panchakshara means "five syllables" — Na, Ma, Shi, Va, Ya. Each syllable corresponds to one of the five elements of creation (earth, water, fire, air, and ether). Om Namah Shivaya is called the Panchakshara Mantra because these five syllables form its core.

How many times should Om Namah Shivaya be chanted?

Traditionally 108 repetitions (one mala) as a daily practice. On Mondays or Maha Shivaratri, 1008 repetitions (ten malas) is considered especially auspicious.

Can beginners chant Om Namah Shivaya?

Yes — it is one of the most accessible mantras in the tradition, requiring no complex ritual or initiation. Sincerity and consistency matter far more than perfection of pronunciation or technique.

What is the best time to chant Om Namah Shivaya?

Brahma Muhurta (before sunrise) and the junction times of sunrise and sunset are considered most auspicious. Monday is the most favourable day. The mantra can also be chanted any time — consistency matters more than timing.

What is the difference between Om Namah Shivaya and Om Namah Shivay?

These are the same mantra — "Shivay" is a common colloquial and North Indian pronunciation of the Sanskrit "Shivaya." Both refer to the same Panchakshara Mantra and carry the same meaning and significance.
