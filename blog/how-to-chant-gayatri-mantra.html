Mantra Guide

How to Correctly Chant the Gayatri Mantra

The Sanskrit, the meaning, precise pronunciation, common mistakes, and how to practise the most revered of Vedic mantras.

The Gayatri Mantra is among the oldest and most sacred mantras in the Vedic tradition — a prayer to Savitr, the divine light of the sun, asking it to illuminate the intellect. It appears in the Rigveda (3.62.10) and is chanted daily by millions. Its 24 syllables are considered so potent that the tradition treats accurate pronunciation as essential. This guide breaks the mantra down word by word and syllable by syllable, flags the most common mistakes, and shows you how to practise it correctly.

The Mantra

In Sanskrit (Devanagari):

ॐ भूर्भुवः स्वः ।
तत्सवितुर्वरेण्यं ।
भर्गो देवस्य धीमहि ।
धियो यो नः प्रचोदयात् ॥

In transliteration (IAST):

Oṁ Bhūr Bhuvaḥ Svaḥ
Tat Savitur Vareṇyaṁ
Bhargo Devasya Dhīmahi
Dhiyo Yo Naḥ Prachodayāt

The three words after Om — Bhūr, Bhuvaḥ, Svaḥ — are the vyahritis, invocations of the three worlds (earth, atmosphere, and heaven). They precede the 24-syllable Gayatri verse proper, which begins at Tat.

Word-by-Word Meaning

Knowing each word makes correct chanting far easier, because you know where one word ends and the next begins:

  • Om — the primordial sound, the absolute.
  • Bhūr — the physical/earthly realm.
  • Bhuvaḥ — the mental/atmospheric realm.
  • Svaḥ — the spiritual/heavenly realm.
  • Tat — "that" (the Supreme, the divine).
  • Savitur — of Savitr, the divine Sun, source of all life.
  • Vareṇyam — most adorable, worthy of being sought.
  • Bhargo — radiance, effulgent divine light.
  • Devasya — of the divine, the radiant one.
  • Dhīmahi — "may we meditate upon / contemplate."
  • Dhiyo — the intellect, our thoughts and understanding.
  • Yo — "who / which."
  • Naḥ — "our, of us."
  • Prachodayāt — "may it inspire / illuminate / impel."

The sense: "We meditate upon the adorable radiance of the divine Sun, the source of all. May that light illuminate our intellect." It is fundamentally a prayer for the awakening and guidance of the mind.

Syllable-by-Syllable Pronunciation

Use these English approximations as a starting point. Keep long vowels (ū, ī, ā) clearly longer than short ones — this is the single most important rule in Sanskrit chanting:

  • bhūr — bhoor (long "oo")
  • bhu·vaḥ — bhu-vah
  • svaḥ — svah (close to "swah")
  • tat — tut
  • sa·vi·tur — sa-vi-TUR
  • va·reṇ·yam — va-REN-yam
  • bhar·go — BHAR-go
  • de·vas·ya — day-VAS-ya
  • dhī·ma·hi — DHEE-ma-hi
  • dhi·yo — dhi-YO
  • yo naḥ — yo nah
  • pra·cho·da·yāt — pra-cho-da-YAAT

Common Mistakes to Avoid

These are the errors that most often creep in — correcting them sharply improves your chanting:

  • Shortening "bhūr." The ū is long — "bhoor," not "bhur" with a short vowel.
  • Softening the aspirated "dh" in dhīmahi and dhiyo. It is an aspirated "d-h," not a plain "d," and the ī in dhīmahi is long.
  • Mispronouncing the retroflex ṇ in vareṇyam — the tongue curls back; it is distinct from a plain "n."
  • Saying "swaha" instead of "svaḥ." The third vyahriti is svaḥ (ending in the soft aspiration visarga), not the svāhā used in fire offerings — a very common confusion.
  • Ending "prachodayāt" too short. The yāt carries a long "aa."
  • Blurring vowel length in song. Musical settings can obscure which vowels are long — chant slowly enough to keep them distinct.
  • Running the vyahritis together. Keep Bhūr — Bhuvaḥ — Svaḥ as three clear, separate words.

How to Practise

  1. Sit comfortably with a straight spine, eyes closed, and take a few slow breaths to settle. If possible, face east, toward the rising sun.
  2. Begin slowly — accuracy before speed. It's better to chant it correctly a few times than quickly many times.
  3. Keep attention on the sound and its meaning — the prayer for the light to illuminate the mind.
  4. Chant a fixed number of repetitions — traditionally 108, counted on a mala or a counter — or for a set time.
  5. When finished, sit quietly for a moment before returning to activity.

How Many Times and When

The traditional count is 108 repetitions — one round of a japa mala. The Gayatri Mantra is classically chanted at the three sandhyas (dawn, noon, and dusk), but dawn is the most important. The most auspicious window is the pre-dawn Brahma Muhurta, when the mind is naturally quiet. A traditional lifetime goal is a purashcharana of 1,25,000 repetitions for the mantra to be fully established in practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most important thing to get right?

Vowel length. Sanskrit distinguishes short and long vowels (u vs ū, i vs ī, a vs ā), and the Gayatri's power is tied to its precise 24-syllable structure. Chant slowly enough to keep each vowel distinct, and the rest follows.

Is it "svaḥ" or "svāhā"?

It is svaḥ — the third of the three vyahritis (bhūr, bhuvaḥ, svaḥ), ending in a soft aspiration. Svāhā is a different word used in fire offerings. Confusing the two is one of the most common Gayatri mistakes.

Can anyone chant the Gayatri Mantra?

Today the Gayatri Mantra is widely and openly chanted, and many teachers hold that anyone may chant it with sincerity and respect. Some traditional lineages prescribe formal initiation (upanayana / guru diksha); follow your own tradition or teacher if that matters to you.

How many times should I chant it?

The traditional count is 108 repetitions, ideally at dawn. Shorter counts of 3, 11, or 27 are also common. Consistency and correct pronunciation matter more than the number.

Can I chant it silently?

Yes — silent mental repetition (manasa japa) is widely practised and considered highly effective. For learning correct pronunciation first, though, chanting aloud or whispered helps you train the sounds accurately before moving to silent practice.
